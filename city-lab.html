The levers, by the D variables of the built environment1

How urban planning works

Plans and zoning

A city sets out where it is going in a comprehensive plan, an ordered process that determines community goals and aspirations in terms of community development.2 Zoning puts it into practice: a municipality divides land into land-use and building "zones", each of which has a set of regulations for new development.3

The D variables

In travel research, urban development patterns have come to be characterized by "D" variables. The original "three Ds," coined by Cervero and Kockelman (1997), are density, diversity, and design, later joined by destination accessibility and distance to transit. The authors add that the Ds are rough categories.1

Density

Planners count homes per hectare. One official plan sets detached houses at 0–20 units per hectare, townhouses and duplexes at 20–40, low-rise apartments at 60–75 and apartments at 100–200.5 Between houses and towers is the missing middle, a term coined by architect Daniel Parolek in 2010.6 UN-Habitat recommends at least 15,000 people per km², that is 150 people/ha for a sustainable neighbourhood.4

Height and floor area

Downtown, heights are set through the floor area ratio: the ratio of a building's total floor area (gross floor area) to the size of the piece of land upon which it is built.7 In this lab, the downtown height lever stands in for it.

Diversity: mixed use

UN-Habitat's third principle: At least 40 per cent of floor space should be allocated for economic use in any neighbourhood, and single function blocks should cover less than 10 per cent of any neighbourhood.4 Ewing and Cervero found that walking is most strongly related to measures of land use diversity, intersection density, and the number of destinations within walking distance.1

Design: the street network

Design includes street network characteristics within a neighborhood, measured in terms of average block size, proportion of four-way intersections, or number of intersections per square mile.1 UN-Habitat's first principle: The street network should occupy at least 30 per cent of the land and at least 18 km of street length per km².4

Distance to transit

Transit-oriented development puts homes and jobs near stations: The densest areas of TOD are normally located within a radius of 1/4 to 1/2 mile (400 to 800 m) around the central transit stop. A half mile (800 m) corresponds to the distance someone can walk in 10 minutes.8

Other kinds of transit

Each mode serves a different width of city. UN-Habitat's table of catchments gives, for each, the stop interval and the corridor width served: bus 200M and 800M, guided bus 300M and 800M, light rail 600M and 1,000M, rail 1,000M+ and 2,000M+. Combining walkability and public transport catchment, the suggested distance between two arterial routes is between 800 to 1,000 m.4

Station spacing

The map spaces stations evenly so it reads well; a real city doesn't. Modelling the best placement of stations, Crampton found that stations should indeed be significantly more dispersed in the suburbs.9

Destination accessibility

Destination accessibility is synonymous with access to trip attractions, and in some studies, destination accessibility is simply represented by distance to the central business district, as it is here. It mattered most for driving: vehicle miles traveled (VMT) is most strongly related to measures of accessibility to destinations.1

Green space: 3+30+300

A rule of thumb launched by Cecil Konijnendijk in 2021: 3 mature trees from every home, 30 percent tree canopy cover in every neighbourhood and 300 metres walking distance from the nearest high-quality public park or other green space.11

Where the city stops

An urban growth boundary is a regional boundary, set in an attempt to control urban sprawl; in the United States, the first urban growth boundary was established in 1958, around the city of Lexington, Kentucky.12 Across 120 cities, built-up densities declined significantly, at an average annual rate of 2.0±0.4 percent, between 1990 and 2000.13

The map and the city

This site's map follows London's: The first schematic Tube map was designed by Harry Beck in 1931, and as a schematic diagram, it shows not the geographic locations but the relative positions of the stations.14 The first lever moves between the two.

Taking part

Planning works best with the people who live in the city. Sherry Arnstein's 1969 ladder runs from non-participation through tokenism to citizen power.15 The IAP2 spectrum has five levels: information, consultation, involvement, collaboration and empowerment.16 A charrette is a collaborative design process that involves all necessary disciplines at key decision points to produce a feasible plan.17 This lab is a small one: shape the city, then share the link to your plan with us.
