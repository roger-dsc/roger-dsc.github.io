        How it works

        What limits a line

        A line carries as many people as its trains hold, times how many trains can run in an hour. How close trains can follow is set at the busiest station: the sum of the controlling dwell time, the minimum train separation, and the operating margin will typically control the line capacity.1 The manual's typical figures assume a 45-s dwell time and 20-s operating margin; we use its chart of separation by train length for each kind of signalling.1

        Fixed blocks

        The track is divided into blocks, and a train may enter a block only when it is empty. A minimum of two empty blocks must separate trains and these blocks must be long enough for the braking distance plus a safety distance. The simplest system can accommodate a throughput approaching 24 trains per hour. With more signal aspects and shorter blocks, conventional train control systems can support a throughput of up to 30 trains per hour.1

        Moving block

        A moving-block signaling system has neither blocks nor aspects. The system is based on continuously or frequently calculating the clear (safe) distance ahead of each train and then relaying the appropriate speed, braking, or acceleration rate to each train. Each train's stopping point will be based on the normal braking distance for that train plus a safety distance.1

        Driverless, like SkyTrain

        For SkyTrain, the biggest decision was to make SkyTrain completely automated – a driverless train – one of the world's first, and the system chosen was SelTrac CBTC, offering unattended driverless operation and moving-block technology.3 Automatic driving can increase total line capacity by 2 to 4 percent, with or without a driver; going without one has demonstrated its economy and safety on numerous automated guideway transit (AGT) systems, and on selected rail systems in Europe and Canada.1 The Expo Line was designed to handle a full MKI, 6 vehicles per train fleet operating at recovery headway of 75 seconds.2

        Bigger trains

        The number of cars in a train is a major determinant of person capacity—the longer the train, the more people it can carry. But station platforms are designed for the longest train the system plans to operate, and are hard to lengthen once built.1 On the Expo Line, the platform length currently provided at most Expo Line Stations is 80 metres.2 Operators count a train's capacity differently, so we give each one as its operator does.4, 5, 6

        Tokyo

        Japan counts a train as 100% full when everyone has a seat or a strap, and publishes how full each line is in its busiest hour. In 2023, the Chūō Rapid line ran 28 ten-car trains an hour from Nakano to Shinjuku, with room for 41,440 and carrying 65,510: 158%. The Yamanote line ran 20 eleven-car trains towards Shinjuku, carrying 42,500: 131%.10 At 150%, commuters have enough personal space to read a newspaper; at 250%, they are pressed against each other, unable to move.11 The Yamanote line was once far fuller: in the 1990s, sections of the line were running over 250% capacity. On it, digital ATC (D-ATC) was introduced from 30 July 2006,13 and JR East's newer ATACS is a moving block ATC system similar to CBTC.14

        The control center

        Automatic train supervision is generally not a safety-critical aspect of the train control system. At its simplest, it does little more than display the location of trains on a mimic board or video screen in the central control or dispatcher's office. In more advanced systems where there is ATO, computer algorithms are used to attempt to automatically correct lateness. Further still, the computer can then adjust terminal departures, dwell times, and train performance to ensure that trains merge evenly without holds. Delays matter most at the busiest times: it is at these times that operating margin and schedule recovery times are most needed to correct service irregularities.1 MTR's control center at Tsing Yi has room to accommodate a larger team of controllers.7
