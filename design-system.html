The idea

The site is a city, and its metro map is how you find your way around.

The city reads like a well-signed station. You always know where you are, and what is next.

One job per sign
The nameboard says where you are. The route strip says where you are on the line. Nothing else competes.
Loudest is where you are
The current station gets the biggest type and the only lit mark. Everything around it stays quiet.
Two ways to read a line
Color and number, every time. A line can be found without reading, and named without seeing its color.
Only what this level needs
Map, then station, then article. Each level answers the question that brought you to it, and offers the next one.
Content first
Signs frame the writing. They never decorate it.
One left edge
Every page begins at the same place, so moving between them feels like staying in one city.

Layout

One left edge. Station pages and the City Lab start at the same place; articles sit in a narrower column for reading.

Line pages · 1260 px
Stations and the City Lab · 1180 px
Articles · 740 px, centered
Side gutter · 16 px

The station pages and the City Lab share the 1180 px column with a 16 px gutter, so the two start at the same left edge at every width. Articles sit in a centered 740 px reading column, about 75 characters a line. On a phone every page keeps the 16 px gutter, and nothing scrolls sideways.

The top bar is the same everywhere: the logo at 28 px, the name at 20 px, the tagline at 12 px, a 60 px bar. On a phone the tagline goes, then the name; the bar stays.

Sound

Quiet unless asked. One chime, then the station, in four languages.

Off until asked
Sound is off by default. The speaker button on the map turns it on, and the choice is remembered.
One chime
The Held chord: three notes of our own, D, E and A, held so they ring together, in a soft bell tone.
Gentle on the ear
Comfort limits are always on: no harsh highs, clicks, fast wobble or loud peaks.
Four languages, one order
English, Cantonese, Mandarin, then Japanese, after the chime.

Tune them in the Chime lab and the Announcement lab, reached from the Transit Lab.

The network and its map

London1908–1933

London Underground

A whole network can read as one system: one symbol, one alphabet, one diagram.

What we took One line color and one badge, repeated everywhere; the map drawn on a grid.

More, and the sources

The bar-and-circle roundel went up on platform nameboards in 1908. Edward Johnston's alphabet, first submitted in 1916, gave the Underground a voice of its own. Harry Beck's diagram, printed in January 1933, drew the network with only horizontals, verticals and 45° lines.

  • London Transport Museum, "The evolution of the roundel"
  • London Transport Museum, "Edward Johnston: the man behind London's lettering"
  • London Transport Museum, "Transforming the Tube map: Harry Beck's iconic design"

New York1972

New York City Subway

The map is a diagram of the service, not a picture of the city.

What we took 45° and 90° angles, even spacing, and one dot per station.

More, and the sources

Unimark International, with Massimo Vignelli, Joan Charysyn and Bob Noorda, issued the diagram map in 1972. It straightened the city to make the routes legible. New York replaced it in 1979 with a geographic map.

  • MoMA, "New York Subway Map, 1970–1972"
  • New York Transit Museum, "Towards a Better Way: The 'Vignelli' Map at 50"

Chicago1993

Chicago "L"

Name the lines by color, and the map becomes the way to talk about the system.

What we took A line's color is its name, not its decoration.

More, and the sources

On February 21, 1993, the CTA replaced destination names such as Howard and Dan Ryan with colors, to help new riders, visitors and people who speak English as a second language. Red, Green and Blue had already marked routes on 1970s maps; the Orange Line opened that October as the first line to arrive with a color name.

  • CTA, "CTA celebrates 25 years of color-coding Chicago's rail lines"
  • WTTW, "How the CTA Map Got Its Colors"

Seoul2023

Seoul Metro

A map for 23 lines has to be redrawn, not extended.

What we took One angle system for the whole network, and two ways to read every line.

More, and the sources

Seoul published its first full redesign in 40 years in 2023. The map moved to an octilinear grid, drew Line 2 as a circle, marked transfers like traffic lights, and added colors and patterns for people with color-vision deficiency. Eye-tracking tests cut the time to find a station by about 55%.

  • Seoul Metropolitan Government, "Redesign of the Seoul Metro map for the first time in 40 years"

São Paulo1974

São Paulo Metro

A network can take its shape from its own logo.

What we took One geometric idea for the whole diagram, carried through every line.

More, and the sources

The Metro opened on September 14, 1974, between Jabaquara and Vila Mariana, the first stretch of the North–South Line (Line 1–Blue) and the first metro line built in Brazil. Its c. 1967 conceptual map, by the São Paulo design office Cauduro Martino, drew the planned network as a diamond that echoed the Metro logo almost exactly.

  • Metrô, "Parabéns Metrô! Há 39 anos facilitando a mobilidade urbana em São Paulo"
  • Metrô, "Linha 1-Azul"
  • Transit Maps, "Original Conceptual Map for the São Paulo Metro, c. 1967"

Medellín2004

Metrocable

A line can be a cable car, if that is what reaches the hills.

What we took A line is defined by who it reaches, not by what runs on it.

More, and the sources

Línea K, a cable car, opened in 2004. The publicly owned Metro company planned it to win riders for its two rail lines and to reach the informal settlements around the city, long neglected. By 2021 Medellín had five cable-car lines in its outlying barrios, all joined to the rest of the network: the rail lines, the bus rapid transit and a tram.

  • ReVista (Harvard), Julio D. Dávila, "Medellín’s Low-Carbon Metrocables" (2021)

Copenhagen2002

Copenhagen Metro

With no driver, a network can run often, and all night.

What we took Show the wait, not a timetable: the next train is never far away.

More, and the sources

The first driverless journeys ran in October 2002, on two fully automated lines. In 2009 the Metro began running 24 hours a day, seven days a week, which few metros in the world offer. Its frequent departures have won awards and been copied many times around the world.

  • Metroselskabet, "20 years of the Metro has transformed the capital from metropolis to metropolis" (2022)

Signs everyone can read

Mexico City1969

Mexico City Metro

A station can be recognized without reading, in any language.

What we took A symbol for the place first, the name second; a number and a color for the line.

More, and the sources

Lance Wyman, with Arturo Quiñones and Francisco Gallardo, gave every station a pictogram drawn from its name or history: a grasshopper for Chapultepec, a duck for Candelaria. The Metro opened in 1969.

  • The Guardian, "The symbolic simplicity of Mexico City's metro signs"
  • Creative Review, "Lance Wyman and the Mexico City Metro"

Tokyo2004–2016

Tokyo Metro and JR East

A number is found faster than a name.

What we took A line badge with a number, read before the station's name.

More, and the sources

Tokyo Metro and Toei numbered their stations from April 1, 2004: one letter per line in the line's color, two digits per station. JR East followed on August 20, 2016, starting at Meguro (JY22) on the Yamanote Line.

  • MLIT, "東京の地下鉄の路線名及び駅名の記号・番号による表示について" (2003)
  • JR East, "「駅ナンバリング」の導入を開始します" (PDF, 2016)

Taipei2014

Taipei Metro

A code can carry a station across languages.

What we took A line letter and a station number, read before the name.

More, and the sources

Station numbering went network-wide with the opening of the Songshan Line on November 15, 2014. Each line code starts with the first letter of its color name in English — G for Green — so a traveler who reads neither Chinese nor English can still find the station.

  • Taipei DORTS, "Taipei MRT signage system design elements and content"
  • Structurae, "Songshan Metro Station (Green Line)"

Singapore2019–2020

Singapore MRT

A sign should work before the words do.

What we took Color, number and symbol first; the name in the reader's own language.

More, and the sources

LTA's signage system made its debut with the first stage of the Thomson–East Coast Line in January 2020, after a three-year review. It relies on colors, numbers and symbols so that language and eyesight are less of a barrier. Station names appear in Singapore's four official languages.

  • LTA, "Wayfinding"
  • MDDI, parliamentary reply on vernacular languages for wayfinding signs

Delhisince 2002

Delhi Metro

Accessibility can be designed in from the first station, not added later.

What we took Tactile paths, Braille at the lift, low counters: the route works without sight or stairs.

More, and the sources

Delhi Metro's first stations were designed for universal access before service began in December 2002, with an access audit at a station still under construction. The ITO station carries the same kit: tactile paths and warning strips at the platform edge, Braille lift buttons inside and out, low ticket counters, wider gates and dedicated toilets.

  • Samarthyam, "Delhi Metro: Mobility for All"
  • ITA-AITES, "ITO Metro Station, New Delhi" (PDF, 2017)

Hong Kongin-car maps

MTR

The journey is shown inside the train, not only on the platform.

What we took The route strip: passed stops gray, one lit dot for here, the line's color throughout.

More, and the sources

MTR's in-car route maps light the station you are at and the stops ahead, show where and which way to change, and say which side the doors will open.

  • Filosophy, "Public transport usability in Tokyo and Hong Kong"
  • Interfaces Institute, "Finding a station without reading"

Washington, DC1968

Washington Metro

Put every sign where the decision is made, and keep the architecture clear.

What we took The nameboard and the exit directory: information at the point of need, nothing stuck on for decoration.

More, and the sources

Harry Weese asked Unimark to keep signs off his vaulted walls, so the system became freestanding pylons. WMATA still uses the Helvetica weight Vignelli specified, and moved to Helvetica Now in 2025.

  • WMATA, "Wayfinding design FAQs"
  • Vignelli Center, "Best of Design: Vignelli Transportation"

Sydney2019

Sydney Metro

A metro with no driver has to tell you everything itself.

What we took Where the train is automated, the sign and the screen carry the reassurance.

More, and the sources

Sydney Metro opened on May 26, 2019, as Australia's first fully automated, turn-up-and-go rail service. Its wayfinding was tested with passengers before construction, using 3D models and VR to check sightlines and platform changes; stations use solar e-paper signs for live bus departures.

  • Alstom, "Sydney Metro commences revenue service" (PDF, May 26, 2019)
  • DWS, "Sydney Metro: customer experience case study"
  • Visionect, "Solar-powered urban signs with live departures"

Beijing1971

Beijing Subway

A name written twice is read by everyone at once.

What we took Chinese and English together, at the same size and weight.

More, and the sources

Work on the first line finished in October 1969; trial passenger service began on January 15, 1971, and the line opened to the public on September 15, 1981, the first metro in mainland China. Beijing’s 2015 municipal standard for urban rail signs says sign text should use Chinese and English together.

  • Beijing Municipal Government, “地铁线路织传奇”
  • Beijing Municipal Bureau of Quality and Technical Supervision, “公共交通客运标志 第2部分：城市轨道交通” (DB11/T 657.2—2015, PDF)

Shanghai1993

Shanghai Metro

One strip can say where you are, where you are going and where you have been.

What we took The current station in the line’s color, the direction ahead, and the stations behind.

More, and the sources

Line 1’s first 6.6 km, from Xujiahui to Jinjiang Park, began operating on May 28, 1993. At its 30th anniversary the network was 831 km, 20 lines and 508 stops; the operator called it the largest metro system among cities. SPUR’s 2011 study noted a platform graphic that marks the station you are in red, the direction of travel black and the stations already passed gray; signs were in English and Chinese.

  • Shanghai Information Office, “Shanghai Metro on the right track after 30 years”
  • SPUR, “Learning From a World-Class Transit System”

Barcelona1924

Barcelona Metro

A line can be read three ways at once: by number, by color, by symbol.

What we took A number and a color for every line, and one mark for the whole system.

More, and the sources

The first section opened on December 30, 1924, when the Gran Metro ran from Catalunya to Lesseps, the embryo of today’s L3. TMB identifies each line by number or color — L1 red, L2 purple, L3 green, L4 yellow, L5 blue — and its red diamond with a white M has long been an icon of the city; the 2023 identity manual calls it a consolidated identity.

  • TMB, “Elements commemoratius del metro”
  • TMB, “Map and plan licensing”
  • TMB, “Sistema d’identitat corporativa de TMB — Manual reduït” (PDF, 2023)

Milan1964

Milan Metro

Write the station’s name all along the wall, so every carriage can read it.

What we took The name repeated where the eye needs it, not left to a single sign.

More, and the sources

Line 1 opened on November 1, 1964, with stations by Franco Albini and Franca Helg and signage by Bob Noorda. Noorda ran a colored band along the station walls, red for Line 1 and green for Line 2, and set the station’s name into it, repeated every five meters, so it could be read from every car, even while the train was moving. The project won the Compasso d’Oro in 1964.

  • Domus, "The design that changed Milan: the 1964 metro system" (2024)
  • nss magazine, "The history of Milan subway’s signage" (2021)

The station as a place

Budapest1896

Millennium Underground Railway

The oldest line in the city is still its most ornamental.

What we took A station can keep its original craft: tiles, ironwork, and a name set into the wall.

More, and the sources

Line 1, the Millennium Underground, opened on May 2, 1896, built by Siemens & Halske for the millennial exhibition. It was the first underground railway in continental Europe and the first to run on electric traction from the start. The stations kept their Zsolnay tiles, and in 2002 the line joined Andrássy Avenue on the UNESCO World Heritage list.

  • UNESCO World Heritage Centre, "Budapest, including the Banks of the Danube, the Buda Castle Quarter and Andrássy Avenue"
  • UNESCO, nomination for the 2002 extension (PDF)

Paris1900–1913

Paris Métro

The way in is part of the identity.

What we took A station page should feel like arriving somewhere: the platform opens before the content.

More, and the sources

Hector Guimard's cast-iron and glass entrances gave the Métro its own style. 86 of the 167 built are protected as historic monuments.

  • RATP, "Do you know Hector Guimard?"
  • MoMA, "Entrance Gate to Paris Subway (Métropolitain) Station"

Buenos Aires1913

Buenos Aires Subte

A metro can begin as a piece of the city's own history.

What we took The first line keeps its first trains: the ride is part of the identity.

More, and the sources

Line A opened on December 1, 1913, between Plaza de Mayo and Plaza Miserere, making Buenos Aires the first city in Latin America with an underground railway. Its Belgian La Brugeoise cars carried passengers until 2013.

  • Argentina.gob.ar, "1 de diciembre – Inauguración del 1er. Subterráneo de Bs. As."
  • Buenos Aires Ciudad, "Línea A"

Moscow1935–1952

Moscow Metro

A station can be public architecture, not a service space.

What we took The station page as a plate: the name is the architecture, the line is the trim.

More, and the sources

The Metro opened in 1935, conceived as people's palaces. Mayakovskaya (1938) lifted its ceiling into mosaics; Komsomolskaya (1952) is the baroque high point of the Stalinist style.

  • Railway Technology, "Moscow Metro stations: the evolving designs of the 'people's palace'"
  • UNESCO, "The triumph of the Stalinist Empire: Komsomolskaya"

Stockholmsince 1957

Stockholm Metro

Art belongs in the everyday journey.

What we took A station should have a surface worth looking at; here, the nameboard and the map are the art.

More, and the sources

Art is in 94 of the system's 100 stations, across 110 km, by 250 artists. The first work went into T-Centralen in 1957; the cave stations of the 1970s left the bedrock exposed.

  • SL, "The world's longest art exhibition"
  • Architectural Digest, "Stockholm's subway is the world's longest art gallery"

Lisbonsince 1959

Metropolitano de Lisboa

A station wall can be a work of art, not a surface for signs.

What we took The station page can carry a surface: pattern and color before the text.

More, and the sources

The first stations opened to the public in 1959, and Maria Keil covered them in azulejo, reviving a Portuguese tradition. She was almost the only artist in the network until the early 1970s; from 1991 the program opened to many national and international artists.

  • Metropolitano de Lisboa, "Arte nas estações"
  • Gulbenkian, "Arte Pública no Metro de Lisboa" (catalog record)

Montreal1966

Montreal Métro

No two stations need the same room.

What we took Each station can have its own architecture, and a work of art made for it.

More, and the sources

The Métro was planned in the early 1960s with a different architect for every station and artworks throughout. A 1963 proposal to set aside part of each station's construction budget for art was ahead of its time; Quebec's 1% policy later made it standard. The collection now runs past ninety works.

  • Radio-Canada, "Le métro de Montréal : galerie d'art souterraine"
  • STM, "New artwork unveiled at Préfontaine station"

Cairo1987

Cairo Metro

The first metro in a region can wear the city's own face.

What we took One mark on the wall can be enough; a station does not need a kit of signs.

More, and the sources

Line 1 began service in 1987 between Helwan and Ramses, 29 km, making Cairo the first city in Africa and the Arab world with a metro. The line now runs 44 km to New El Marg. The entrance in Old Cairo carries a single red M.

  • Cairo Metro, "First line"
  • SYSTRA, "Cairo Metro – Egypt"

Dubai2009

Dubai Metro

A driverless network can still give every station a theme.

What we took A station can belong to one of a few elements and still be one system.

More, and the sources

The Red Line opened on September 9, 2009, and every train runs without a driver; RTA describes the network as the world's largest fully automated metro. Station interiors follow four themes — air, earth, fire and water — each with its own palette.

  • RTA, "Dubai Metro & Tram guide" (PDF)
  • Gulf Construction, "RTA unveils Metro interior designs"

Vienna1978

Vienna U-Bahn

A new line can reuse the stations the city already loves.

What we took Keep the old building; let the new service run through it.

More, and the sources

Vienna’s first underground line, U1, opened on February 25, 1978, between Karlsplatz and Reumannplatz. The U4 runs on Otto Wagner’s Stadtbahn; eight of its stations keep their historic appearance almost 130 years after they were built, and Schönbrunn and Stadtpark are fully preserved.

  • Wiener Linien, “120 years of public transport in Vienna”
  • City of Vienna, “40 Jahre U-Bahn: Die U4 – von der Stadtbahn zur U-Bahn”
  • WienTourismus, “Otto Wagner”

Riyadh2024

Riyadh Metro

A new network can open whole, with its busiest interchange as a landmark.

What we took The interchanges get a stronger mark than the stops around them.

More, and the sources

King Salman inaugurated the network on November 27, 2024, and its six lines, 176 km with 85 stops, opened between December 1, 2024, and January 5, 2025. Every train runs driverless, and every stop has platform doors. At King Abdullah Financial District, where the Blue, Yellow and Purple lines meet, Zaha Hadid Architects wrapped the interchange in a lattice of opposing sine waves that echoes the patterns desert winds leave in sand.

  • Urban Transport Magazine, "Officially inaugurated: A complete new network of metro lines for Riyadh" (2024)
  • Zaha Hadid Architects, "King Abdullah Financial District (KAFD) Metro Station"

Melbourne1885

Melbourne trams

The street can be the network.

What we took A stop belongs to the street it stands in.

More, and the sources

Melbourne’s trams date from 1885. Today they are the world’s largest operational tram network: more than 250 kilometers of double track across 24 routes, running on roads shared with the traffic around them.

  • Transdev, "Yarra Trams: the world’s largest light rail network"

One look, everywhere

Switzerland1944–1980

Swiss Federal Railways

One clock and one grid can hold a multilingual country together.

What we took One grid for every page, and signs that work before the words do.

More, and the sources

Hans Hilfiker's station clock was standardized in 1944; its face was redrawn in 1952 and the 1955 version is still in use. From 1978, Josef Müller-Brockmann and Peter Spalinger built SBB's information system on a simple grid, with Helvetica and pictograms.

  • Museum für Gestaltung Zürich, "SBB Bahnhofsuhr"
  • Museum für Gestaltung Zürich, "SBB CFF FFS visual information system"
  • Swiss National Museum, "The iconic Swiss station clock"

Berlin1990–1994

Berliner Verkehrsbetriebe

A divided network, reunited, can be given one voice.

What we took One color for the city, one typeface for every sign.

More, and the sources

In the early 1990s, after the Wall, BVG and MetaDesign gave the two halves of the network one identity: yellow, and FF Transit, a sans serif drawn by MetaDesign after Frutiger, for every sign and notice. The work is documented in the book Berlin ist Gelb.

  • German Design Council, "Berlin Ist Gelb – Oder: Der Schönste Job meines Lebens"
  • Typografie.info, "Welche Hausschrift benutzt Berliner Verkehrsbetriebe (BVG)?"

Germany2005

Deutsche Bahn

A railway can speak in one typeface, from the platform to the timetable.

What we took One sans serif for every voice, in three weights only.

More, and the sources

DB Type arrived in 2005. DB Sans, drawn by Christian Schwartz with Erik Spiekermann, carried signs, timetables, advertising and the in-train magazine; DB Neo began replacing it in 2021.

  • Christian Schwartz, "DB Sans"
  • Fonts In Use, "DB Sans"

Toronto1954

Toronto Subway

One typeface and one colored circle can carry a whole system.

What we took A single sans serif for every sign, with the line’s color beside its number.

More, and the sources

The Yonge subway opened on March 30, 1954, Canada’s first subway. The TTC’s 2023 signage standards make Swiss 721 BT the primary typeface and give each line a number and a color — Line 1 yellow, Line 2 green, Line 4 purple — with colored circles as the second identifier.

  • TTC, “60 Years Yonge”
  • TTC, “Signage and Wayfinding Standards” (PDF, November 2023)

Vancouver1985

SkyTrain

Give every service one color, and never let it change.

What we took One fixed color per line, repeated on every map, sign and screen.

More, and the sources

SkyTrain opened on December 11, 1985, as the world’s first fully automated, driverless metro; its original line was named the Expo Line as a legacy of Expo 86. TransLink gives each service one color everywhere — Expo blue, Millennium yellow, Canada teal — so the color itself tells you what you are looking at.

One letter can stand for the whole system.

What we took One mark for the network, and a color for each line, chosen for how it reads.

More, and the sources

In 1965 the MBTA unveiled a color for each of its rapid-transit lines, the work of the design firm Cambridge Seven Associates, with all seven of its founders on the project. The colors were chosen for how they looked before they were given to lines; the familiar stories of Harvard’s crimson and the harbor are not entirely true. The firm found that many European systems were branded with a single letter and none in the United States had used a T, so Boston got the T.
